Goodies

Asking well
in conversation.

The golden rule: show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context on the task and ask them to follow it. If they would be confused, Claude will be too.

The six basic techniques

They apply to all current models, and the order matters: the first three do half the work.

01

Be clear and direct

Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows. The more precisely you explain what you want, the better the result.

Be specific about the output format and the constraints. When the order or completeness of steps matters, give them as a numbered list. And if you want work that goes above and beyond, ask for it — do not rely on the model to infer it from a vague prompt.[1]

02

Say why

Providing the motivation behind an instruction — explaining why the behaviour matters — helps Claude understand your goal and deliver a more targeted response. It is smart enough to generalise from the explanation, and so to handle the cases you did not anticipate.[1]

03

Show examples

This is the most reliable way to steer the format, tone and structure of a response. Three to five examples give the best results. They should be relevant — close to your actual case —, diverse — covering edge cases, and varied enough that Claude does not pick up an unintended pattern — and structured, wrapped in tags so it can tell them apart from instructions.[1]

Worth knowing: you can ask Claude to evaluate your examples for relevance and diversity, or to generate more from the ones you have.
04

Structure with tags

When a prompt mixes instructions, context, examples and variable input, tags like <instructions>, <context>, <input> remove the ambiguity. Keep tag names consistent across prompts, and nest them when the content has a natural hierarchy.[1]

05

Give it a role

A single sentence is enough to focus behaviour and tone for your use case. "You are an assistant specialising in French contract law" is not decoration: it decides the vocabulary, the level of detail and the caution reflexes.[1]

06

Say what to do, not what to avoid

Negative phrasing works poorly. Replace "do not use markdown" with "write in smoothly flowing prose paragraphs". Another lever: the style of your prompt influences the style of the response — removing markdown from your own message reduces markdown in the answer.[1]

When you paste a large document

Beyond 20,000 tokens — a hundred pages or so — the order of the pieces changes the result.

Order

Document first, question last

Put long documents at the top of your message, above the query, the instructions and the examples. Putting the query at the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in Anthropic's tests, especially with complex, multidocument inputs.[1]

This is the only rule on this page with a number attached. It is also the one almost nobody applies, because you naturally write your question before pasting your file.
Grounding

Have it quote before it answers

On a long document, ask it to first extract the relevant passages, then carry out the task. This focuses it on the useful content and helps it ignore the rest — and it hands you the means to check the answer.[1]

Tagging

Several documents, several tags

With multiple sources, wrap each in a <document> tag holding its content and its source. Without that separation, two contradictory documents become one undifferentiated mass and Claude can no longer attribute the claims.[1]

Knowing when chat is no longer the right place

A good prompt does not rescue the wrong tool.

The boundary

Chat is for when the output is a thought

Stay in conversation if what you want fits in a few exchanges: a question, an explanation, a brainstorm, an idea to pressure-test.

As soon as the expected result is a file someone will open, as soon as the task touches several files or several apps, or as soon as you would describe it as a task rather than a question, chat will have you copy-pasting in both directions for an hour.[2]

→ See Delegating with Claude Cowork for that case.
A word on response length. Recent models are more concise and more direct by default, and may skip the summary after a series of actions. If you want visibility into what was done, ask for it explicitly — it is a sentence to add, not a setting to find.[1]
