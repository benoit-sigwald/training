One 45-minute session · before module 00 · no prerequisite

Understanding where AI comes from prevents two symmetrical mistakes: believing everything is new, and believing nothing has changed. This session is not about reciting dates. It is about knowing what a model can do, what it cannot, and why.

Seventy years, a dozen names.

The same project — getting a machine to do what takes intelligence — has changed its label every time the previous one lost its value.

1805 — 1900

Predicting with numbers · the real ancestors

Long before any machine, statistics invented the tools today's AI still runs on. Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805; Gauss used it to find the dwarf planet Ceres again after it was lost from view — the first quantitative prediction confirmed by observation.

Late in the century, Francis Galton studied family heights and coined the word regression — "regression towards the mean" — then correlation, taken up and formalised by Karl Pearson.[13] A century later, training a model still means minimising a squared error.

Least squares · regression · correlation
1904 — 1950

Weather, the first prediction machine · the true ancestor

In 1904, Vilhelm Bjerknes framed a decisive idea: forecasting the weather is an initial-value problem. Know the state of the atmosphere now and the equations governing it, and you can compute tomorrow's. That is the definition of a predictive model.

In 1922, Lewis Fry Richardson actually tried it, by hand, in Weather Prediction by Numerical Process. He cut the atmosphere into cells and solved the equations cell by cell. It took him weeks of arithmetic for a few hours of forecast — and the result was wrong. He then imagined a "forecast factory": a hall of 64,000 human computers working in parallel under a conductor. That is, word for word, a description of a modern computing centre, thirty years before one existed.[8]

In March 1950, Charney, Fjørtoft and von Neumann produced the first numerical weather forecast on a computer, on ENIAC. Twenty-four hours of coherent forecast — but weeks to produce it. The first operational services followed in the early 1960s.[9]

Numerical forecasting · modelling · simulation
1963

Lorenz finds the limit · by accident

Edward Lorenz restarted a weather simulation, typing his starting values rounded to three decimals instead of six. The trajectory diverged completely. He had just discovered sensitivity to initial conditions — the butterfly effect — and with it a truth that holds for every model: even a perfect one, with infinite computing power, has a horizon of predictability beyond which it says nothing.

It is the most honest answer to give when someone asks what a model cannot do. It is not a matter of the next version.[10]

Chaos · butterfly effect · limit of predictability
1943 — 1955

Cybernetics · before the words "AI" existed

McCulloch and Pitts proposed a mathematical model of the neuron in 1943. In 1948, Norbert Wiener published Cybernetics and set out the idea of machines that regulate themselves through feedback. In 1950, Alan Turing wrote Computing Machinery and Intelligence and replaced "can a machine think?" with something observable: the imitation game.[12]

Cybernetics · thinking machines
1956 — 1973

The name is invented · and the first promises made

Summer 1956, Dartmouth conference: John McCarthy coins artificial intelligence, partly to break away from cybernetics. Rosenblatt presented the perceptron in 1957, the first learning neural network. In 1966, ELIZA imitated a psychotherapist with a handful of rules — and already convinced users, which remains the field's oldest lesson about our tendency to read intention into a machine.[12]

Promises far outran results. In 1969, Minsky and Papert demonstrated the limits of the simple perceptron: connectionism froze for fifteen years.[12]

Artificial intelligence · connectionism
1974 — 1980

First AI winter

The Lighthill report (1973) judged results disappointing against the funding. Budgets were cut in the United Kingdom, then the United States. "Artificial intelligence" became hard to say in front of a funder — the first devaluation of the label.[12]

The word is avoided
1980 — 1987

Expert systems · AI that finally pays

The comeback came through industry: rules written by hand by experts, in a narrow domain. MYCIN diagnosed infections, XCON configured computers at DEC and saved tens of millions of dollars. Japan launched its "fifth generation" programme. This was the first AI genuinely deployed in business.

Its weakness was structural: every rule written by hand, and nothing worked outside its domain.

Expert systems · symbolic AI · knowledge engineering
1987 — 1993

Second AI winter

The market for specialised machines collapsed; expert systems proved expensive to maintain. Second devaluation of the word. Researchers renamed themselves: they no longer did AI, they did machine learning, pattern recognition, knowledge-based systems.

The word becomes a liability
1993 — 2011

Data takes over · and statistics changes its name

The paradigm flipped: instead of writing the rules, you have them learned from examples. This was the decade of machine learning, data mining and KDD. In 1997, Deep Blue beat Kasparov — through computing power, not understanding.

On the business side the lineage runs parallel: business intelligence in the 1990s, then big data from 2008 when volume became the central problem.

Machine learning · data mining · business intelligence · big data
1974 · 2001 · 2008

The term "data science" · born three times

Peter Naur proposed "data science" in 1974 as another name for computer science, in Concise Survey of Computer Methods. In 2001, the statistician William S. Cleveland published Data Science: An Action Plan for Expanding the Technical Areas of the Field of Statistics and made it a discipline in its own right. Around 2008, at LinkedIn and Facebook, the job title data scientist appeared for people who were neither statisticians nor computer scientists.[11]

Worth keeping in mind: data science is not the ancestor of generative AI, it is its cousin. One explains and predicts from your data; the other generates text from a model pre-trained on data that is not yours. Confusing the two is the most common framing error in business.

Data science · data scientist
2012 — 2016

Deep learning · the moment it works

2012: AlexNet crushed the ImageNet competition using graphics cards. Three ingredients finally converged: data at scale, affordable computing power, and algorithms known since the 1980s but until then unusable. In 2016, AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol at Go, ten years earlier than expected.

Deep learning · neural networks
2017 — 2022

Transformers · and public access

2017: the paper Attention Is All You Need introduced the Transformer architecture, which made training on text possible at an unprecedented scale. BERT followed, then the GPT family. On 30 November 2022, ChatGPT opened the technology to the general public — and it is that date, not a scientific breakthrough, that tipped companies over.[12]

Large language models · LLMs · generative AI
2023 — 2026

Agents · where we are now

Models stopped merely answering: they call tools, read files, chain steps. The vocabulary multiplied again — agentic AI, expert agents, copilots. Hub France IA notes that each company picks its own terminology depending on its deployment strategy.[3]

In parallel the framework arrived: the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) was adopted, and its Article 4 has made AI literacy mandatory for employers since 2 February 2025.[7]

Agentic AI · expert agents · copilots

Full circle

In 2023, GraphCast — a learned model from DeepMind published in Science — beat the best operational forecasting system in the world on the large majority of measures, and produced a ten-day forecast in under a minute, where a physical model needs hours of supercomputer time. Between 2025 and 2026, these models moved from experiment to production.[6][1]

Put differently: the first application of computer-based predictive modelling, the one that justified building the first computing centres, has just been taken over by machine learning. Richardson was right about the method. He was missing the machine, and a hundred years.

What the timeline teaches

The field has changed its name a dozen times in seventy years, almost always after a disappointment. When you hear a new word — agentic, copilot, sovereign AI — the right question is not "what does it mean?" but "what does it do that the previous one did not?". Often, nothing.

Why now, and not in 1990.

The ideas ran thirty years ahead of the means. Three curves crossed.

1Data

The web, then smartphones, then sensors. Backpropagation was known in 1986; what was missing was billions of examples to feed it.

2Compute

Graphics cards, designed for video games, turned out to be perfect for training neural networks. AlexNet ran on two consumer GPUs in 2012.

3Architecture

The Transformer, in 2017, finally allowed training on text to be parallelised. Without it, no models with hundreds of billions of parameters.

Three cautions before quoting these figures

Gains concentrate where work is structured and measurable. They are markedly smaller on tasks requiring deeper reasoning. The same study can therefore show +50 % in one job and nothing at all in another.[1]

The gain is not evenly shared. The strongest effect falls on the least experienced; it is close to zero for the most skilled. That is not a detail: it changes who in a team has the most to gain from being trained first.[5]

There is a documented downside. Employment for software developers aged 22 to 25 has fallen nearly 20 % since 2024, and one third of organisations expect to reduce headcount within the year. Recent work also suggests heavy reliance on AI may slow skill acquisition over the long run.[1]

One figure I will not give you

You will read everywhere that AI cuts drug development time by 20, 40 or 75 %. I found no solid source for it. Those percentages travel from blog post to blog post with no published study behind them.

What is established, by contrast: the FDA authorised 258 AI medical devices in 2025, but only 2.4 % of those relying on clinical studies are backed by randomised trials — the vast majority go through device-modification pathways resting on existing evidence.[1] That is exactly the nuance a round number erases.

The rule holds beyond this example: when a percentage is attached to no named study, do not repeat it. That is module 07 applied to your own communication.

What actually comes out: a distribution, not an answer

A language model does not pick a word: it assigns a probability to every word it knows, then draws one. Compare the two cases.

The mechanismThe limit it explains
The output is a distribution: there is always a most probable word, even when the right answer does not exist.Limit 01 — it does not know that it does not know. There is no "I don't know" output: that is not an oversight by the designers, it is the shape of the system.
The weights are frozen when training ends. Nothing you write modifies them.Limit 02 — it does not know your company. And limit 04 — it has no memory: what looks like memory is text handed back to it each time.
A neuron performs a weighted sum, not a symbolic arithmetic operation. The model learned what a correct calculation looks like.Limit 03 — it does not compute, it imitates computation. Hence the duty to recompute totals, or to give it a tool that genuinely calculates.
The weights encode the regularities of the training corpus, including the ones we would rather it had not learned.Limit 05 — it reproduces the biases in its data. Bias is not added afterwards: it is in the weights.
The next word is drawn from the distribution, not always taken from the top.Limit 06 — it is not deterministic. Same request twice, two answers. Adjustable, not removable.
Each layer composes what the previous one detected; depth builds increasingly abstract representations.This is where the power comes from — and why nobody can say precisely why one answer came out rather than another.

Two words you will hear, now defined

Backpropagation. The training method: compare the output to the right answer, measure the error, then travel back through the network adjusting every weight slightly in the direction that reduces that error. Repeated billions of times. Known since 1986, unusable until the data and the graphics cards arrived.

Open weights. When a lab publishes the numerical values its model learned, anyone can download it and run it on their own machines. That is what separates GLM-5.2 or DeepSeek from models reachable only through a provider's interface — and it is the only realistic route if your data must not leave.

What it runs on.

The weights and layers of the network do not float in a vacuum. They live in a building, on machines, fed by an electricity grid. Eight layers, from the ground up to your screen.

Your application

The interface where you type: browser, mobile app, a module inside your business software. It is the only layer most users will ever see.

What it imposes — everything underneath is invisible to you, including where the processing actually happens.

The API

The technical entry point: authentication, quotas, per-token billing, logging. This is where it is decided who may call what, and at what price.

What it imposes — with no cap set here, a badly written loop can burn a monthly budget overnight.

The inference service

The software that runs the model: it batches requests from several users to keep the graphics processors busy, caches identical conversation openings, and compresses the weights to go faster.

What it imposes — this is the layer that explains why the same model can cost five times less at one provider than another.

Orchestration

The conductor: it spreads jobs across thousands of machines, restarts what fails, and isolates customers from one another.

What it imposes — this is where the promise of separation between your data and another customer's is actually kept, or not.

The internal network

A very large model does not fit on a single card: it is split across dozens of processors that must talk to each other constantly, at enormous bandwidth. It is often the network, not the compute, that sets the speed.

What it imposes — you cannot simply "add more cards": they have to be wired together, and that is the hard part.

Compute

The graphics processors and their very high bandwidth memory. This is the scarce, expensive, concentrated component: global capacity has grown 3.3× every year since 2022, and a single Taiwanese foundry fabricates almost every chip running frontier AI.[1]

What it imposes — hardware dependency is the most concrete point of fragility in the whole chain.

Cooling

All the electricity consumed ends up as heat. It has to go somewhere: moved air, outside air where the climate allows, or liquid brought directly against the chips — increasingly unavoidable as cards run hotter.

What it imposes — water. Annual inference water use for a single consumer model may exceed the drinking water needs of 1.2 million people.[1]

Site and electricity

Land, a building, and above all a grid connection — the real limiting factor today. Installed power capacity for AI data centres has reached 29.6 GW, comparable to New York state at peak demand.[1]

What it imposes — you do not build a data centre where you want it: you build it where there is power available.

What it costs the planet.

Two scales never to be confused: what your request costs, and what the whole thing costs.

0.24Wh per text request

Figure published by Google for a median Gemini prompt, alongside 0.03 g of CO₂e and 0.26 mL of water. Compare with 0.2 to 0.3 Wh for a conventional web search.[14]

0.34Wh, another estimate

The order of magnitude stated publicly by OpenAI. A reasonable range for a text request is therefore 0.24 to 0.34 Wh.[14]

72,816tonnes of CO₂e

Estimated emissions from training Grok 4 alone, in 2025. Training is a one-off cost, but a colossal one — and increasingly undocumented.[1]

≈ 945TWh by 2030

International Energy Agency projection for global data centre electricity consumption — roughly double today's level.[15]

How to read these figures without getting them wrong

At the scale of one request, the impact is small. A question to an assistant costs about as much as a web search. Making an employee feel guilty for asking three questions a day makes no sense: their consumption is lower than that of their screen being on for the same minute.

At global scale, the impact is massive. These are not decided by the same people. The total comes from the number of requests times the number of users, and from training the models — not from your individual use.

The useful lever in a company is not using AI less, it is not sending thirty pages with every question. That is exactly step 09 of the data path: what inflates the bill also inflates consumption. Turning down the effort dial and trimming the context acts on both at once.

Three reasons to stay careful with these figures

Some of them come from the vendors themselves. The 0.24 Wh study is internal to Google, its methodology is debated, and independent audits are being called for. Scope matters enormously: does it count cooling? idle machines? amortised training?

Transparency is going backwards. Training code, parameter counts, dataset sizes and training duration are no longer disclosed for several of the most resource-intensive systems, at OpenAI, Anthropic and Google alike.[1] In other words: the cost of a request is willingly published, the cost of training much less so.

Sources contradict each other about the present. For global data centre consumption in 2025, you will find both 485 TWh and 787 TWh depending on the scope used. Projections to 2030 converge; the starting point does not. Always quote the source with the figure.

What a model cannot do.

These limits are not bugs waiting for the next release. They follow from how the models are built.

LIMIT 01

It does not know that it does not know

A model produces the most plausible text, not the truest. It has no internal mechanism to flag that it is making something up. This is what we call a hallucination — and it is the number one risk cited by micro-businesses, SMEs and mid-caps alike in the Hub France IA survey.[2]

Consequence — every figure, name, date and quote must be checked. That is module 07.

LIMIT 02

It does not know your company

It was trained on public data up to a cut-off date. Your prices, contracts, procedures and clients: it knows nothing about them. If it still answers precisely, it is inventing.

Consequence — give it the context, or connect it to your data. That is modules 09 and advanced 04.

LIMIT 03

It does not compute, it imitates computation

A language model predicts words. A total, a percentage or a computed date come out by resemblance, not arithmetic — unless it has a tool that genuinely calculates.

Consequence — always recompute a total. That is module 08.

LIMIT 04

It has no memory by default

Outside a configured workspace, every conversation starts from zero. What looks like memory is context handed back to it each time, within a limited window.

Consequence — one workspace per recurring topic. That is module 09.

LIMIT 05

It reproduces the biases in its data

Whatever is over-represented in the training corpus is over-represented in the answers. Mid-caps list bias reproduction among their two main risks.[2]

Consequence — particular caution on anything involving people: hiring, appraisal, selection.

LIMIT 06

It is not deterministic

The same request can produce two different answers. That is a property, not a defect — but it rules out building a critical process on a single unchecked answer.

Consequence — for repeatable use you need a fixed request template and a check. That is module 10.

Myths and reality.

Six statements you hear in meetings, and what the figures say.

The myth

"AI is going to replace my team's jobs."

The reality

The benefits companies actually report are time saved and productivity gained — not headcount removed. What disappears are tasks: formatting, first drafts, summaries. The work shifts towards checking and deciding.

The myth

"It's a bubble, it will pass like blockchain."

The reality

The field has already been through two winters, in 1974 and 1987 — so the objection is legitimate. The difference is usage: between 52 % and 91 % of generative AI projects reach production depending on company size, and users report satisfaction of 3.5 to 4 out of 5. That is not the profile of a technology nobody uses.[2]

The myth

"You have to be a large company to get started."

The reality

Micro-businesses and SMEs go from idea to production in 0 to 3 months, against 6 months for mid-caps. Their obstacle is not size, it is the lack of technical expertise — cited first by both.[2]

The myth

"Agents will automate everything on their own."

The reality

Gartner forecasts that over 40 % of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027. Many products presented as agents are in fact assistants with no tools, no rights to act and no orchestration. Autonomy is not the absence of supervision.[3]

The myth

"The top model in the ranking is the right choice."

The reality

Going from 51 to 61 points of intelligence multiplies cost per task by roughly seven. For most writing, support or summarising work, the quality gap does not justify the bill. The right question is the minimum acceptable score for your task.[4]

The myth

"AI understands what it is saying."

The reality

It predicts the most likely continuation. ELIZA already convinced users in 1966 with a few rewriting rules: our tendency to read intention into a machine is older than the technology. Worth knowing before trusting it.

Two rankings, two different questions

Artificial Analysis puts models through exams; Arena has humans vote on anonymised answers. The first measures capability, the second preference. If your use is conversational — support, writing, everyday assistance — look at Arena. If it is technical or agentic — code, document analysis, extraction — look at Artificial Analysis. And remember that no ranking measures your job.[4]

AI literacy obligation

Article 4 of the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) requires organisations deploying an AI system to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff. The obligation has applied since 2 February 2025, with no size threshold and regardless of risk level — using a conversational assistant is enough to trigger it.

National authorities can enforce and fine from 2 August 2026. This programme and its attendance sheet are the documentary evidence of that training.

The CRAFT matrix.

Five ingredients. It is the core of module 02, and the only model worth memorising.

CContext

The situation, the "why". A company that is growing and one in crisis do not get the same answer.

RRole

Who the tool should be. "You are an experienced HR manager" sets the level of expertise.

AAction

The expected deliverable, with a clear verb: write, summarise, analyse, compare.

FFormat

The shape of the output: email, table, six slides. Without it, the tool decides for you.

TTone

The register: professional, warm, direct. This is what makes the text ring true.

Prerequisite

This track only opens after module 07 of the foundation track — "Check before you send" — and after a real deliverable. An agent does not make fewer mistakes: it makes them faster and with no witness.

The slash, two natures.

The core of module 00. Not everything that starts with "/" is the same kind of thing.

1Built-in command

Fixed logic, hard-coded in the CLI. It does one precise thing, with no reasoning from the model, and cannot be customised. /clear, /compact, /model, /cost, /help.

2Skill

A prompt-based capability: invoking it loads a Markdown instruction file into the context, which Claude then executes — with arguments, dedicated tools and possible subagents. .claude/commands/ still works, .claude/skills/ is the recommended approach.

Three environments, three behaviours

Claude CodeThe full palette: context (/clear, /compact, /context), navigation (/resume, /branch, /rewind), model (/model, /effort), code (/plan, /diff, /security-review).
ConnectorsA connected MCP server exposes its prompts as /mcp__server__prompt. Plugins expose theirs as /plugin:command.
claude.aiA much smaller palette: /new clears the context, /help lists what exists. Everything else after a "/" is either the name of a skill, or plain text.

Two distinctions that cost time: /cd moves the session — a later /resume finds it from the new directory — whereas /add-dir grants access without moving, which is what you want in a monorepo. And /branch switches to a copy of the conversation while preserving the original, which you return to with /resume.
